# Ahmed (2019) — Preliminary Geological and Geochemical Study of Clay at Al-Harur Area, Abyan Governorate, Yemen

**Full citation:** Ahmed, N.M.M. (2019). "Preliminary geological and geochemical study of clay at Al-Harur area, Abyan governorate, Yemen." *University of Aden Journal of Natural and Applied Sciences*, Vol. 23, No. 2, pp. 425–441. DOI: 10.47372/uajnas.2019.n2.a13. Author affiliated with the Department of Archaeology, Faculty of Arts, University of Aden (stated specialty: geology of metal and non-metal mineral resources). Bilingual (English/Arabic) publication in a regional, non-internationally-indexed university journal.

**Type of paper:** This is exactly the **cautionary secondary indicator** already flagged in your `references.md` — a geochemistry-only (no XRD, no CEC, no rheology) reconnaissance study of a **different, much lower-grade Yemeni clay occurrence**, in a **fundamentally different depositional setting** than your Alaslaf deposit. Its main value to your thesis is as a **contrasting/counter-example**, not as a corroborating or methodological source.

## Geological setting and method

**Location:** Al-Harur village, Abyan Governorate, ~12 km WNW of Jaar town (13°13′54.9″–13°14′8″ N, 45°11′38.5″–45°12′30″ E), covering ~1.28 km². **Geological context: Quaternary (Upper Pleistocene to Holocene) fluvial, alluvial, alluvial-eolian, and eolian deposits** — loess-loam material reworked and transported by wadi flows and wind, *not* an in-situ volcanic-ash-alteration deposit. **This is a critical genetic distinction from typical bentonite deposits** (which normally form by in-situ devitrification/alteration of volcanic glass or tuff): the clay here is detrital/reworked sedimentary material, so even where montmorillonite is present, its origin story is entirely different from what you'd expect for Alaslaf (presumably a primary volcanic-derived deposit, per Alshameri 2013). Don't cite this paper as genetic corroboration for Alaslaf's formation mechanism.

**Sampling:** 6 hand-dug pits (depths ranging 4.0–7.0 m), 28 total samples (including one composite sample per pit representing the whole logged depth), full bulk-oxide ("full silicate") chemical analysis: SiO₂, Al₂O₃, TiO₂, Fe₂O₃, MnO, CaO, MgO, Na₂O, K₂O, SO₃, Cl, P₂O₅, LOI (Table 1). **No XRD, no CEC, no swelling index, no viscosity or any drilling-fluid-relevant test was performed anywhere in this study.** Clay-mineral identity is inferred **purely by comparing bulk oxide percentages to generic reference compositions** for kaolinite, montmorillonite, illite, chlorite, and glauconite (Table 2, sourced from Read's 1962 mineralogy textbook) — a much weaker evidentiary basis than direct diffraction or exchange-capacity measurement.

## Key results

**Average bulk chemistry across the 28 samples:** SiO₂ ≈48.7%, Al₂O₃ ≈14.1%, Fe₂O₃ ≈7.1%, TiO₂ ≈0.82%, CaO ≈10.4%, MgO ≈3.55%, Na₂O ≈0.82%, K₂O ≈2.15%, LOI ≈11.6%.

**Mineral identification reasoning:** the author argues the SiO₂/Al₂O₃ pattern is closer to china clay (kaolinite) than ball clay in general shape, but since **Al₂O₃ (14.1%) falls far short of kaolinite's typical ≥30%**, and since the CaO/MgO content is judged "compatible" with the reference montmorillonite composition in Table 2, the clay is classified as **montmorillonitic**.

**⚠️ Flag worth noting if you ever cite this paper's mineral identification:** the paper's own reference table (Table 2) lists "typical" montmorillonite as having **Fe₂O₃ = 0.83%** — but the Al-Harur samples average **Fe₂O₃ ≈ 7.1%, roughly 8–9× higher** than that reference value. The paper does not address or explain this large mismatch; it focuses its justification on the CaO/MgO axis of comparison while silently passing over a Fe₂O₃ value that, on its own logic, would argue *against* a clean montmorillonite identification. Since **no XRD was run to confirm the mineralogy independently**, this is a real interpretive weak point — treat the "montmorillonite" identification here as a plausible but **chemistry-inferred, not directly confirmed**, classification.

**Grading/suitability conclusion (the paper's actual bottom line):** the clay is explicitly graded **"low grade"** due to high impurity-oxide content (SiO₂, Fe₂O₃, TiO₂, CaO, MgO, Na₂O all higher than "pure clay" reference values) and is recommended **only as a raw material for cement production** — the paper makes **no claim whatsoever about drilling-fluid or bentonite-industry suitability**; that possibility isn't raised or tested anywhere in the text.

## Numbers worth citing directly (with appropriate hedging)

- Average bulk chemistry: SiO₂ ≈48.7%, Al₂O₃ ≈14.1%, Fe₂O₃ ≈7.1%, CaO ≈10.4%, MgO ≈3.55%, Na₂O ≈0.82%, K₂O ≈2.15%
- Using this study's own averages, a rough (Na₂O+K₂O)/(CaO+MgO) mass ratio ≈ (0.82+2.15)/(10.4+3.55) ≈ **0.21** — well below Karagüzel (2010)'s "mixed bentonite" threshold of 0.57 — i.e., **if** this clay is montmorillonitic at all, this ratio would place it as strongly Ca-dominated (a "hard" Ca-bentonite requiring more intensive activation than a mixed Na-Ca bentonite), a useful rough comparative data point for your Chapter 1 regional-context table, clearly labeled as chemistry-inferred and not independently confirmed
- Deposit graded "low grade," recommended use: cement raw material only

## Caveats / limitations

1. **No XRD or direct mineralogical confirmation anywhere** — the "montmorillonite" classification rests entirely on comparing bulk oxide percentages to a generic textbook reference table, which is a materially weaker method than the diffraction- and exchange-capacity-based characterizations in your other core sources (Alshameri 2013, Karagüzel 2010, Bahranowski 2021).
2. **Unaddressed internal inconsistency**: the sample's average Fe₂O₃ (≈7.1%) is roughly 8–9× the paper's own reference "montmorillonite" Fe₂O₃ value (0.83%), a discrepancy the paper doesn't discuss — worth flagging if you ever cite this paper's mineral identification as settled.
3. **Fundamentally different depositional/genetic setting** (Quaternary alluvial/eolian reworked sediment vs. presumed in-situ volcanic-derived origin for Alaslaf) — don't use this paper to support any claim about Alaslaf's formation mechanism, even if both are eventually described as montmorillonite-bearing.
4. **No drilling-fluid, bentonite-industry, CEC, swelling, or rheological data of any kind** — the paper's own conclusion recommends cement raw material only; it makes no bentonite-suitability claim, so don't cite it (even loosely) as supporting evidence for drilling-mud applications.
5. **Regional, non-internationally-indexed journal, single author, non-geology home department** (Archaeology, Faculty of Arts) — a lower-authority source than your peer-reviewed international journal sources; treat its findings as a useful regional data point rather than a rigorously vetted mineralogical study.
6. Sampling is reasonably systematic for a preliminary study (6 pits, 28 samples, composite + interval sampling) but shallow (max 7 m) with no lateral-continuity or reserve-estimation analysis — appropriate for the "preliminary" framing the paper itself uses, not for a deposit-scale characterization.
