# Hassan & Abdel-Khalek (1998) — Beneficiation and Applications of an Egyptian Bentonite

**Full citation:** Hassan, M.S., Abdel-Khalek, N.A. (1998). "Beneficiation and applications of an Egyptian bentonite." *Applied Clay Science*, 13(2), 99–115. DOI: 10.1016/S0169-1317(98)00021-0. Received 9 June 1997; revised 14 April 1998; accepted 14 April 1998. Authors affiliated with Central Metallurgical Research and Development Institute (CMRDI), Helwan, Cairo, Egypt.

**Deposit/location:** Gabal Hamdal locality, Abu-Zeneima area, Sinai, Egypt. Host unit: Nukhul Formation (Lower Miocene, Burdigalian age) — shallow marine deposit. Six trench samples collected (MJ-48, MJ-50, MJ-51, MJ-52, MJ-53, MJ-56). Two bentonite clay beds, 1.5–3 m thick each, with 0.5–2 m Quaternary overburden.

**Method:** Full mineralogical/chemical/thermal characterization (XRD bulk + oriented mounts, chemical analysis, DTA/TG) of run-of-mine (ROM) ore → mechanical beneficiation (crushing → attrition scrubbing → hydrocyclone classification → dilute HCl leaching) → alkaline activation (1–5 wt% Na₂CO₃) for drilling-mud application, and separate acid activation (HCl) for bleaching-clay application → rheological benchmarking against API/OCMA drilling-fluid specs and commercial reference bentonites (Wyoming/Cedars, Meycogel, Thailand) → cost-benefit/economic feasibility analysis.

## ⚠️ Scope correction vs. current `references.md` entry

Your `references.md` (and my earlier bibliographic-only verification pass) characterized this as a purely cautionary "counter-example: poor Na-exchange response, low smectite purity." **Having now read the full paper, that framing is only half the story and should be corrected.** Yes, the ROM ore is genuinely poor (low grade, Ca-Mg dominant, poor natural Na-exchange, <50% smectite) — but the paper's actual conclusion is that **after beneficiation + Na₂CO₃ activation, the final concentrate successfully meets drilling-mud specification and even surpasses some standard requirements.** This makes Gabal Hamdal a **success-after-treatment case**, not a failure case — structurally closer to Magzoub (2014) and Al-Homadhi (2007) than to a discouraging precedent. This is actually a *more* useful and *more* optimistic precedent for your own Alaslaf risk framing than the one-line description suggested. Recommend reframing this citation in Chapter 1 accordingly: **use it to show that even quite poor-grade (<50% smectite), strongly Ca-dominant starting material can still be brought into spec with a well-designed beneficiation + activation flowsheet** — supporting evidence for feasibility, with appropriate caveats about processing complexity (see below).

## Key findings

**Site/deposit description:** The Nukhul Formation bentonite occurs as two beds (1.5–3 m thick) under thin Quaternary cover — broadly comparable in scale to the "several-meter, locally exposed" character of your Alaslaf outcrops described in Alshameri (2013), useful as a general comparator for outcrop-scale expectations.

**Mineralogy (XRD):** ROM samples show calcite as the dominant gangue mineral (confirmed by very high CaO: 19.7–36.9 wt%, and high loss-on-ignition: 20.0–30.8%), with quartz, dolomite, and kaolinite as accessory phases. The clay is "typical divalent-cation saturated smectite" — i.e., Ca-Mg dominant, not Na-dominant, in its natural exchange state.

**Basal spacing (XRD):** d₀₀₁ of ROM smectite ranges **14.45–15.31 Å**, expanding to **16.28–16.31 Å with ethylene glycol treatment** (standard smectite-swelling diagnostic test). This 14.45–15.31 Å range is very close to what Alshameri (2013) reported for natural Alaslaf bentonite (15.3 Å) — **useful cross-deposit confirmation that this basal-spacing range is a reliable Ca-dominant-smectite signature you can cite alongside your own future XRD results.**

**Structural formula (average, assuming trivalent Fe) — the single most quotable number in this paper for your Na/Ca argument:**

Na₀.₀₅Ca₀.₁₅K₀.₀₄(Al₁.₃₄Fe³⁺₀.₄₃Mg₀.₂₄)(Si₃.₉Al₀.₁)O₁₀(OH)₂

This shows exchangeable **Ca is 3× exchangeable Na** (0.15 vs 0.05 per formula unit) — a clean, directly citable, structurally-derived (not just bulk-oxide) demonstration of Ca-dominance, exactly the kind of exchangeable-cation-basis evidence that (as flagged in the Alshameri summary) bulk oxide ratios cannot properly provide. This is a good methodological model: if you can obtain a structural formula for your own purified Alaslaf material, it is more defensible than a raw Na₂O/CaO wt% ratio.

**CEC and surface area:** Methylene-blue CEC of ROM samples = **40–43 meq/100g** — at the *low end* of the commercial bentonite range (40–120 meq/100g, per Taylor 1985, cited in-paper). Surface area of the smectite fraction ≈ 800 m²/g (other clay minerals <40 m²/g; non-clay minerals <5 m²/g) — the large total surface area for the smectite-only fraction is consistent with genuine (if minor) smectite presence despite low bulk grade.

**Estimated smectite content:** ROM samples <50% smectite — historically considered marginal/uneconomic on its own, but shown in this paper to be salvageable through beneficiation.

**Beneficiation flowsheet (directly reusable as a process-design reference for your Chapter 3):**
1. Primary crush (jaw crusher) to 100% below 5 mm
2. Attrition scrubbing: 50% solids, 2500 rpm, 1 h (Denver D12 flotation cell used as attritioner) — disintegrates smectite from gangue
3. Classification: 2″ Mozley hydrocyclone, 10% solids, 30 psi
   - Underflow (coarser than 44 µm, ~15 wt% of feed): heavily contaminated with quartz/calcite — discarded
   - Overflow (finer than 44 µm, ~85 wt% of feed): taken forward as bentonite pre-concentrate
4. Acid leaching of pre-concentrate: 0.5 M HCl, 1 h, 2500 rpm (removes residual calcite) → **final concentrate**
5. Activation: alkaline route (1–5 wt% Na₂CO₃, mixed into 350 mL slurry with 22.5 g clay — **note this is essentially the identical activation protocol/slurry ratio used by Magzoub 2014**, suggesting this is a fairly standardized regional/industry test protocol worth adopting for comparability) for drilling-mud application; separate acid route (HCl) for bleaching-clay application.

**Beneficiation results (chemical, Table 1 — SiO₂/TiO₂/Al₂O₃ wt% progression):**

| Sample | SiO₂ | TiO₂ | Al₂O₃ | CaO |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ROM range (MJ-48…MJ-56) | 31.0–40.7 | 0.03–0.46 | 5.6–7.6 | 19.7–36.9 |
| Pre-concentrate | 42.40 | 0.83 | 11.6 | (declining) |
| Final concentrate | 51.01 | 1.15 | 15.4 | **1.77 (minimum)** |
| Clay fraction (<2 µm) reference | 50.40 | 1.25 | 15.6 | – |

CaO drops from ROM levels as high as 36.9% down to a final concentrate value of **1.77%** — this is the clearest single number demonstrating the beneficiation flowsheet's effectiveness at removing carbonate gangue, and Al₂O₃ climbing from 5.6–7.6% (ROM) to 15.4% (final concentrate) tracks the corresponding smectite enrichment.

**Swelling index:** ROM samples: 20–25 (low grade). After Na₂CO₃ activation at 4–5 wt%, swelling index exceeded 100 — a substantial improvement, though the paper notes this still falls short of the swelling indices of the highest-grade reference smectites used industrially (unspecified numeric ceiling given).

**Rheology of activated final concentrate:** Plastic viscosity of activated clay concentrates = **9–10 cP**, which the authors note "fall well within the oil-grade bentonite requirements" — specifically bracketed by 5 cP (Meycogel reference) and 11.0 cP (Wyoming reference, "distinguished" commercial standard). ROM (untreated) samples, by contrast, showed viscometer dial reading at 600 rpm "well below the minimum required value of 30 cP," and filter-loss values "higher than the maximum standard limit" — i.e., **ROM material fails on both viscosity and filtration control, while the beneficiated + activated final concentrate meets or approaches standard specifications.** (Note: the exact numeric comparison table, Table 4, extracted with some OCR/formatting corruption in the source PDF — worth re-checking the original table directly if you need the precise dial-reading/filtrate numbers for the final concentrate vs. Wyoming/Thailand references; the prose-stated PV figures above are reliable, the raw table digits are not fully trustworthy as extracted.)

**Bleaching application (secondary use-case, not directly relevant to your thesis but useful context):** Bleaching ability rose from 20% (ROM) → 55% (pre-concentrate) → >90% (final concentrate, acid-activated with 6 N HCl for 300 min).

**Cost-benefit analysis — useful for your Section 8 economic framing:**
- Alkaline-activated (drilling-mud-suitable) product: ~US$22/t without beneficiation vs. ~US$30/t with beneficiation
- Acid-activated (bleaching-clay) product: ~US$21.2/t without beneficiation vs. ~US$27.5/t with beneficiation
- Compare to imported activated clay prices generally **US$200–400/t** (bleaching clays) and specialty products even higher
- Authors' conclusion: beneficiation adds only modest processing cost (because it reduces downstream acid/alkali consumption in the activation step, partially offsetting the extra beneficiation cost) while producing a substantially better/more marketable product — a clean, directly citable import-substitution economics argument.

## Numbers worth citing directly

- Structural formula showing Ca:Na ≈ 3:1 in exchange sites: Na₀.₀₅Ca₀.₁₅K₀.₀₄(Al₁.₃₄Fe³⁺₀.₄₃Mg₀.₂₄)(Si₃.₉Al₀.₁)O₁₀(OH)₂
- d₀₀₁ basal spacing: 14.45–15.31 Å (ROM), expanding to 16.28–16.31 Å with ethylene glycol
- MB CEC: 40–43 meq/100g (low end of 40–120 meq/100g commercial range)
- CaO: ROM 19.7–36.9% → final concentrate 1.77% (post-beneficiation)
- Al₂O₃: ROM 5.6–7.6% → final concentrate 15.4%
- Swelling index: ROM 20–25 → activated (4–5% Na₂CO₃) >100
- Activated concentrate PV: 9–10 cP (cf. Meycogel 5 cP, Wyoming 11.0 cP)
- Bleaching ability: 20% (ROM) → 55% (pre-concentrate) → >90% (final concentrate)
- Beneficiation yield: ~85 wt% of feed retained as pre-concentrate (hydrocyclone overflow), ~15 wt% discarded (underflow)
- Estimated processing cost: US$21–30/t vs. US$150–400/t imported

## Relevance to this thesis

- **Chapter 1 (balanced risk framing):** re-cite as a **qualified success story** rather than a pure counter-example — a <50% smectite, strongly Ca-dominant, calcite-rich deposit was successfully upgraded to meet drilling-mud-relevant rheological targets through a defined beneficiation + activation sequence. Pairs well with Magzoub (2014) as two independent "yes, Ca-bentonite can be fixed" precedents from different countries, strengthening your thesis's feasibility argument for Alaslaf.
- **Chapter 3 (Methodology):** the beneficiation flowsheet (attrition scrubbing + hydrocyclone classification + dilute HCl leaching) is a more industrially-scaled alternative purification route than the simple gravity-sedimentation method you and Magzoub both use — worth citing as an alternative/more rigorous purification method if your committee asks about scale-up beyond bench purification.
- **Chapter 3 / methodological cross-check:** the 22.5 g clay / 350 mL slurry Na₂CO₃ activation protocol matches Magzoub (2014) almost exactly — this appears to be a de facto regional standard test protocol; adopting the same ratio in your own Phase 1 activation trials would make your results more directly comparable to both precedents.
- **Section 8 (Economic feasibility):** directly citable cost-per-ton figures and import-substitution logic, structurally identical to the argument you'll want to make for Yemen.

## Caveats / limitations

1. **Correct the "counter-example" framing** in your Chapter 1 draft/references.md — this paper is better used as a success-after-treatment precedent, not a purely cautionary one (see scope correction above).
2. Table 4 (the direct drilling-fluid-spec comparison table, ROM vs. pre-concentrate vs. final concentrate vs. Wyoming/Thailand references) extracted with significant OCR/formatting corruption from the source PDF — the prose-stated PV figures (9–10 cP) are reliable, but do not cite exact dial-reading or filtrate numbers from the raw table without re-checking the original PDF page directly.
3. This paper predates modern RSM/DOE methodology — activation trials are simple incremental dosing (1–5% Na₂CO₃ in fixed steps), not a designed experiment, same limitation noted for Magzoub (2014) and the same opportunity to state your Box-Behnken approach as a methodological improvement.
4. The paper's primary economic/technical framing is Egypt-specific (Egyptian tariffs, Egyptian import context) — useful as a structural template for your own Yemen-specific economic argument, but the actual dollar figures are not directly transferable without updating for current prices and Yemen's own cost structure.
5. Different depositional setting from Alaslaf (Nukhul Formation shallow marine Miocene sediment vs. Alaslaf's Triassic rhyolitic tuff-derived bentonite per Alshameri 2013) — useful as a process/methodology comparator, not as a geological analog.
