# Afolabi et al. (2017) — Properties and Application of Nigerian Bentonite Clay Deposits for Drilling Mud Formulation: Recent Advances and Future Prospects

**Full citation:** Afolabi, R.O., Orodu, O.D., Efeovbokhan, V.E. (2017). "Properties and application of Nigerian bentonite clay deposits for drilling mud formulation: Recent advances and future prospects." *Applied Clay Science*, 143, 39–49. DOI: 10.1016/j.clay.2017.03.009. Received 28 Nov 2016; revised 3 Mar 2017; accepted 8 Mar 2017. Authors affiliated with Covenant University (Petroleum Engineering and Chemical Engineering departments), Ota, Ogun State, Nigeria.

**Deposit/location:** Not a single-site study — this is a **review article** synthesizing decades of research across essentially all known Nigerian bentonite deposits (Afuze/Edo State, Pindiga/Gombe State, Yola/Adamawa State, Abakaliki/Ebonyi State, Ubakala/Abia State, Fika/Benue Trough, Niger Delta, Abbi/Delta State, and ~15 other locations).

**Method:** Literature review/synthesis (no new experimental data generated). Covers: historical background of Nigerian bentonite mining and drilling-fluid use; reserve estimates by region; compiled mineralogical/elemental characterization data (Tables 3–5); compiled rheological/filtration performance data from ~20 prior studies (Table 6); survey of beneficiating agents and additives used across the literature; discussion of rheological models applicable to bentonite drilling muds; and a "future research directions" section identifying gaps (rheological modeling, nanotechnology, HPHT behavior, economic/beneficiation cost optimization).

## Key findings

**National reserve picture (Section 2.1, Tables 1–2):** Nigerian bentonite reserves modestly estimated above **700 million metric tons** nationally, with the single largest deposit at **Afuze, Edo State (~70–80 million metric tons)**. A 2010 RMRDC estimate breaks reserves down further: Abia State cluster (Bende, Umuahia, Arochukwu, Ikwuano, Isiukwuato) 5.8–7.5 million tons; Imo State cluster (Oru, Isu, Orlu, Okigwe) 5.8–7.5 million tons; Borno State cluster (Ngala, Gamboru, Dikwa, Mongunu, Marte) 700 million tons. Additional deposits at Ogurude, Ohaozara, Awka, Jega, Itu are noted as **not yet reserve-estimated** — a useful comparator for your own Alaslaf reserve-quantification gap (also currently unresolved per your Phase 0 checklist).

**Historical arc (Section 2.2):** Nigerian bentonite mining began in 1961 (Bende, Abia State), peaking at 5,106 tons/day production 1961–1963, then declined due to reduced research investment and civil-war disruption; research interest was "rejuvenated" only in the late 1980s, with sodium-beneficiation techniques (Omole et al., 1989) marking the turning point. This is a clean historical-arc narrative parallel worth citing in your Chapter 1 alongside the Khan (2017) and Magzoub (2014) "why is a huge indigenous resource still import-substituted" framing.

**Mineralogy across ~20+ studied deposits (Section 3, Tables 3–5):** The review's central, most load-bearing conclusion: **Nigerian bentonite clays are, almost without exception across every deposit studied, low-grade calcium montmorillonite** requiring beneficiation to become drilling-grade. Marine shale units hosting these deposits (Agwu, Fika, Imo, Dukamaje/Kalambaina formations) are stated to contain **>80% montmorillonite enriched in calcium with little or no sodium section**. This is now a fourth independent national context (alongside Saudi/Khulays, Egypt/Gabal Hamdal & El-Fayoum, Pakistan/Jhelum-Jundola) converging on the same "naturally-occurring bentonite deposits are overwhelmingly Ca-dominant; Na-activation is the norm, not the exception" pattern — a genuinely strong cross-regional argument for your Chapter 1/2 feasibility framing for Alaslaf.

**Al₂O₃/SiO₂ ratio as a lightweight screening diagnostic:** Across the compiled Nigerian dataset, this ratio ranges **0.23–2.48**, "compares favorably" with the standard Wyoming bentonite ratio of **0.38**. This is the same simple oxide-ratio heuristic used in Khan (2017) — worth noting as a recurring but low-rigor screening tool across your reference base, not a substitute for XRD/CEC.

**Falode et al. (2008) result, as reported here (Section 4)** — useful preview since Falode (2008) is on your own to-summarize list: optimum clay concentration 80 g bentonite/350 mL; beneficiation with 12.5% potash+starch and 40 g/350 mL Na₂CO₃ gave **64% increase in swell volume, 750% increase in plastic viscosity, 3000% increase in yield point**; starch and potash gave **35% and 32% reduction in fluid loss** respectively, and **55% and 33% reduction in mud-cake thickness** respectively. **Cross-check these figures directly against the original Falode (2008) paper when you summarize it** — the mud-cake-thickness reduction percentages (55%/33%) are suspiciously identical to the cake-thickness reduction percentages already reported for Khan (2017)'s starch/CMC HV treatment (also 55%/33%) — worth verifying this isn't a citation/transcription mix-up somewhere in the literature chain, since it would be an unlikely coincidence for two independent studies on different clays to produce numerically identical percentages.

**OFAT vs. DOE — directly relevant to your methodological novelty claim (Section 7.1):** The review explicitly states that **most Nigerian bentonite beneficiation research uses One-Factor-At-a-Time (OFAT)** approaches, which "does not account for interaction effects but only accounts for the main effects of the factors being considered." It then explicitly credits a small number of exceptions that used actual Design of Experiment (DOE)/factorial approaches: **Makinde et al. (2011)** (modeled temperature/aging-time effects on rheology), **Oyegoke (2013)** (factorial design for rheological/filtration optimization), and **Abdulkadir et al. (2013)** (kaolinite-based mud, DOE approach). This is a **directly citable, third-party-authored statement of the exact OFAT-vs-DOE gap** you've been building your own methodological novelty argument around across Magzoub, Hassan, El-Mahllawy, and Khan — worth citing this review specifically for that framing since it's an independent, published acknowledgment of the same gap rather than your own inference.

**Future research directions flagged by the review (Section 7):** rheological/thixotropic modeling of Nigerian bentonite muds (noting most work stops at Bingham/Power-Law two-parameter models, with little uptake of Herschel-Bulkley, Robertson-Stiff, or other 3-parameter models); nanotechnology/nanoparticle additives (flagged as essentially unexplored in the Nigerian context specifically, despite global uptake); HPHT/high-temperature behavior of beneficiated Nigerian clays (bentonite dispersions begin to flocculate above 300°F — largely unstudied for these deposits); and **economic/cost-benefit analysis of beneficiation** — explicitly noted as covered by only ~1% of surveyed literature (Joel & Nwokoye, 2010), a good citable "this is an under-studied gap" data point for your own Section 8 economic framing.

## Numbers worth citing directly

- National Nigerian bentonite reserve: >700 million metric tons; largest single deposit Afuze, Edo State (~70–80 million tons)
- Marine shale host formations reported >80% montmorillonite, calcium-enriched, "little or no sodium section"
- Al₂O₃/SiO₂ ratio range across Nigerian deposits: 0.23–2.48 (cf. Wyoming bentonite 0.38)
- Researcher focus breakdown (per Agwu et al. 2015, cited within this review): ~44% rheological analysis, ~40%+ mineralogical characterization, ~1% economic/cost analysis, 0% nanotechnology-specific work (Fig. 1 pie chart)
- OFAT identified as the dominant methodology; only 3 cited exceptions using DOE/factorial design (Makinde et al. 2011; Oyegoke 2013; Abdulkadir et al. 2013)
- Bentonite dispersion flocculation onset: above 300°F (HPHT relevance)

## Caveats / limitations

1. **This is a synthesis/review, not a primary data source** — every specific number in it is a second-hand report of another study's results. Don't cite specific figures (Table 6 rheology values, Table 3–5 mineralogy) directly from this review if you can instead get them from the original paper — treat this as an index/screening tool.
2. **Table 6 contains at least one likely transcription error** (Osadebe et al. 2011 row shows a PV value implausibly identical to an unrelated SiO₂ percentage reported elsewhere in the same review) — a general warning to independently verify any number you might want to pull from this table.
3. **The Falode (2008) figures reported here (55%/33% mud-cake-thickness reduction) are suspiciously identical to the same percentages already reported for Khan (2017)'s starch/CMC treatment** — worth explicitly reconciling when you obtain and summarize the actual Falode (2008) paper, since this could be either a genuine coincidence, a citation-chain error in this review, or (less likely) a citation-chain error already present in Khan (2017) itself.
4. No new experimental or mineralogical data of its own — all conclusions are downstream of the ~30+ underlying studies it cites, many of which are unpublished theses or conference papers not independently available to you; treat any specific claim from an underlying study you don't have access to with appropriate caution.
5. Entirely Nigeria-focused — useful as a cross-regional comparator (a fourth national precedent for the "Ca-dominant clay, needs beneficiation" pattern), not a geological analog to Alaslaf's Triassic rhyolitic-tuff origin.
