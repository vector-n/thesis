# Agwu, Okon & Udoh (2015) — A Review of Nigerian Bentonitic Clays as Drilling Mud

**Full citation:** Agwu, O.E., Okon, A.N., Udoh, F.D. (2015). "A Review of Nigerian Bentonitic Clays as Drilling Mud." SPE-178264-MS, Society of Petroleum Engineers. Presented at the Nigeria Annual International Conference and Exhibition, Lagos, Nigeria, 4–6 August 2015. Authors affiliated with University of Uyo and Afe Babalola University, Nigeria.

**Type of paper:** Literature review / synthesis — not an original experimental study. Chronicles ~40 individual studies on Nigerian bentonitic clays from 1964–2014, tabulates their locations, methods, elemental compositions, rheological results, and major findings, then draws cross-cutting conclusions and identifies research gaps.

**Method:** Narrative + tabular synthesis. Five main tables: (2) study metadata — researcher, year, area, number of locations, experiments done, beneficiating materials used; (3) elemental/oxide analysis results across studies; (4) rheological properties of beneficiated clays across studies; (5) major findings per study. Plus a national map (Fig. 3) and a bar chart (Fig. 4) quantifying what fraction of researchers did rheological analysis vs. mineral characterization vs. both vs. economic analysis.

---

## Historical framing (useful narrative/context material)

Nigerian bentonite research is periodized into three phases:
- **Pioneering years (1959–1962):** first mining/pulverizing at Isieke Ihekie, Abia State (1961), 3000 bags/day capacity; early montmorillonite occurrences reported at Ropp (Plateau) and in Niger Delta sediments.
- **Years of slow activity (1960s–late 1990s):** import substitution by US bentonite stalled local research; the Nigeria–Biafra civil war (1967–1970) is cited as a further disruptive factor.
- **Awakening years (2000–present):** Shell Petroleum Development Company drilled **over 600 wells using Nigerian clay in 2001**, catalyzing renewed research interest; RMRDC's 2010 national reserve assessment (Table 1 in the paper) is the most-cited reserve dataset.

**National reserve figures (RMRDC, 2010, their Table 1):** Borno State clay reserves estimated at **700 million tonnes**; Abia and Imo States each **5.8–7.5 million tonnes** (proven/inferred); several other states (Cross River, Akwa Ibom, Anambra, Gombe, Adamawa/Taraba, Edo, Kebbi) listed as "not yet quantified" or "needs further investigation" — i.e., the national reserve base is still poorly constrained outside a couple of well-studied states.

## Cross-study synthesis — the paper's central finding

**Unanimous conclusion across all ~40 studies reviewed: Nigerian bentonitic clays are predominantly Calcium-based (Ca-montmorillonite) and require beneficiation to meet API bentonite standard.** None of the raw/unbeneficiated Nigerian clays surveyed meet API specification without treatment. This directly corroborates the same Ca-dominance pattern already established across your other local-clay sources (Hassan 1998, El-Mahllawy 2013, Khan 2017, Al-Homadhi 2007, Falode 2008) — this review paper is useful as an aggregate statement of that pattern across an entire national research literature (~40 studies), not just individual deposits.

**Compositional benchmark (Al₂O₃/SiO₂ ratio):** across the reviewed Nigerian clays, this ratio ranges **0.23–2.48**, compared to Wyoming bentonite's **0.38**. Most Nigerian deposits cluster below or near the Wyoming value, consistent with generally lower alumina (i.e., lower true clay-mineral) content relative to silica/quartz impurity load — structurally the same pattern flagged in your Falode (2008) summary for the Pindiga deposit specifically.

**LOI range across all studies:** 1.6–20.19% (very wide spread, reflecting large variation in clay purity/organic-and-hydrate content across deposits).

**Beneficiating materials used across the literature (Table 2):** overwhelmingly **Na₂CO₃** (sodium carbonate), followed by starch, potash, CMC, PAC, trona, and various local materials (sweet potato starch, tannin, Burnt Palm Head Sponge Powder, Akanwu powder/local trona). Na₂CO₃-based sodium activation is by far the dominant beneficiation strategy in the entire reviewed literature — reinforcing that this is the default first-line treatment for Ca-bentonite in this genre of study, consistent with your own thesis's planned activation protocol.

**Methodological gap analysis (Fig. 4, quantified):**
- ~44% of studies did only elemental/mineralogical characterization (XRD/XRF)
- ~41% did only rheological analysis
- Only **~11%** of studies did **both** mineralogical and rheological characterization on the same sample set
- Only **one single study** (Joel & Nwokoye, 2010) in the entire reviewed literature performed an **economic analysis**

This is a directly citable, quantified methodological gap: the overwhelming majority of Nigerian bentonite studies characterize *or* test rheology, rarely both together, and economic feasibility analysis is almost entirely absent from the literature. Your thesis's integrated characterization + RSM-based rheological/filtration benchmarking + planned economic feasibility summary (Phase 4 of your roadmap) directly addresses this exact gap, and this paper gives you a citable, quantified basis for that "gap in the literature" argument.

**Geographic distribution:** most studies concentrated in Northern Nigeria (Adamawa, Gombe, Kaduna, Sokoto, Yobe, Borno states); only one study identified in the South-West. Northern Nigeria (esp. the Pindiga Formation area, Gombe State) is characterized as the most heavily studied and richest bentonitic clay field in the country.

**Design methodology critique:** the review explicitly flags that essentially all beneficiation studies in the literature used a **one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT)** approach, which cannot capture two-factor (interaction) effects — the review's authors state this as a limitation of the existing literature. This is a strong, directly citable justification for your thesis's RSM/Box-Behnken multi-factor design as a methodological advance over the existing Nigerian (and by extension broader West African) bentonite literature.

## Suggested future research directions (from the paper's own conclusions — useful for framing your Chapter 1/2 "gap" narrative)

1. Combine mineral characterization and rheological study on the same deposit in one integrated piece of work (rarely done — only ~11% of reviewed studies)
2. More economic analysis comparing local beneficiation costs vs. imported bentonite costs (almost entirely absent — 1 study only)
3. Viscoelastic/thermal stability testing under HTHP and deepwater conditions (not addressed in the reviewed literature)
4. Determine optimal beneficiating-additive dosages needed to standardize each specific local deposit, and the shelf life of treated clays
5. Better-constrained national reserve estimates (many states listed as "not yet quantified")
6. Compatibility of local clays with different additives under varying thermodynamic conditions

## Numbers worth citing directly

- SPDC drilled >600 wells with Nigerian clay in 2001 (Emofurieta, 2001, as cited by Agwu et al.)
- Nigerian bentonite consumption ~50,000 tonnes/year, historically 100% imported from the USA (Odumugbo, 2005, as cited)
- Borno State reserve estimate: 700 million tonnes (RMRDC, 2010)
- Al₂O₃/SiO₂ ratio range across Nigerian deposits: 0.23–2.48 (vs. Wyoming 0.38)
- LOI range across all reviewed studies: 1.6–20.19%
- Methodological gap: only ~11% of studies combined mineralogy + rheology; only 1 of ~40 studies did economic analysis
- Drilling fluids represent ~41.9% of total oilfield chemical expenditure ($18.2 billion total, Freedonia/Caenn 2013)

## Relevance to this thesis

- **Chapter 1/2 (literature review framing):** this is your single best source for a quantified "gap in the literature" argument — most Nigerian (and by extension, regional West African/Middle Eastern) bentonite studies are siloed (characterization-only or rheology-only), rarely combine both, almost never include economic analysis, and almost universally use OFAT rather than multi-factor DOE. Your thesis's integrated characterization + RSM + economic-feasibility design directly targets all three gaps identified here — good material for justifying your methodology as an advance on the state of the art, not just for Yemen but relative to the broader genre of "local clay vs. API bentonite" studies.
- **Chapter 1 (comparator context):** confirms, at a much larger sample size (~40 independent studies) than any single paper in your reference base, that Ca-dominance + beneficiation-dependency is the near-universal pattern for non-Wyoming/non-US bentonite deposits — strengthens the "expect Ca-dominance, plan for Na-activation" framing already established from your other sources.
- **Introduction (market/economic framing):** useful citable figures for opening paragraphs on global drilling fluid market size and import-substitution economics (Freedonia/Caenn figures, Nigerian import tonnage) — parallels the kind of framing you may want for Yemen's own import dependency, if such figures exist or can be estimated.
- **Chapter 3 (methodology justification):** the explicit OFAT critique is a ready-made, citable justification for choosing Box-Behnken/RSM over the single-factor sweep approach used in nearly all_of the individual studies reviewed here (including several already in your own reference base — Falode 2008, Khan 2017, Hassan 1998 are all OFAT designs).

## Caveats / limitations

1. **This is a secondary/tertiary source** — a review of other studies, not original data. Any numbers cited from it (e.g., specific oxide percentages, rheology values from Tables 3–4) should ideally be traced back to and cited from the original primary study where practical, the same practice you're already applying elsewhere (e.g., preferring Falode 2008 directly over Khan 2017's reused figures).
2. **Coverage is admittedly incomplete** — the authors explicitly state in their conclusions that unpublished or in-review works may not have been captured, and the review only covers studies through 2014.
3. Several of the ~40 studies tabulated are unpublished B.Sc./B.Eng. theses (Bindei 1987, Onize 2003, Lafia 2010, Ademibawa 1999) or hard-to-access technical reports (RMRDC 1990/1991) — not independently verifiable primary sources, so treat any figures traced only to this review (rather than to a publicly available original paper) with appropriately lower confidence.
4. No mention of Yemen, Saudi Arabia, or any deposit outside Nigeria — purely a national-scope review, useful only as comparator/methodological context, not as a geological analog for Alaslaf.
5. Table values in this review occasionally contain internal inconsistencies from the source studies themselves (e.g., the Ahmed et al. 2012 Pindiga entry in Table 3 shows two different value sets separated by "*", seemingly reflecting a raw vs. beneficiated distinction that isn't clearly labeled in the review's own table) — worth spot-checking against the RMRDC/Ahmed 2012 primary source if any of these specific numbers are to be cited directly.
