# Khan et al. (2017) — Improvement of Locally Available Raw Bentonite for Use as Drilling Mud

**Full citation:** Khan, K., Khan, S.A., Saleem, M.U., Ashraf, M. (2017). "Improvement of locally available raw bentonite for use as drilling mud." *The Open Construction and Building Technology Journal*, 11, 274–284. DOI: 10.2174/1874836801711010274. Received 24 May 2017; revised 6 July 2017; accepted 24 July 2017. Open access (Bentham Open). Authors affiliated with King Faisal University (Hofuf, Saudi Arabia), University of Engineering and Technology Taxila, and CIIT Abbottabad (Pakistan).

**Deposit/location:** Seven bentonite sources across Pakistan (Attock, Cherat, Gari Chundun, Jhelum, Azakhel, Jhandola/Jundola, Bimber) sampled for chemical screening; two — **Jhelum** and **Jundola** — selected for full drilling-mud investigation.

**Method:** XRF chemical screening of all 7 sources → sample prep (crush → sun-dry → oven-dry → grind → sieve to No. 200/75 µm per API 13A) → mud preparation at increasing clay concentrations (21, 40, 60, 80, 100 g/350 mL) for Jhelum and Jundola, benchmarked against commercial Mill Gel at 21 g/350 mL (6 wt%, the standard API reference loading) → rheological testing (Fann-type Viscometer Model 900: PV, YP, AV, gel strength) and API filter-press filtrate-loss testing on raw clays → beneficiation trials on both bentonites at fixed 21 g/350 mL (6 wt%) using xanthan gum (XG), carboxymethyl cellulose high-viscosity grade (CMC HV), starch, and XG+CMC / XG+starch combinations, benchmarked against API specification.

---

## Why this is your stated "primary methodological template"

This is the paper your proposal and references.md already flag as the closest methodological analogue, and having read it in full that assessment holds up well. It's a clean, simple two-deposit raw-vs-commercial comparison using exactly the workflow you're extending: local Ca/Na-Ca clay → benchmark against Mill Gel/API → beneficiate with polymers at fixed concentration to close the gap. Its biggest limitation relative to your own design is methodological rather than conceptual: **every additive trial here is one-factor-at-a-time (one additive varied while base clay concentration is held fixed at 21 g/350 mL)** — there is no multi-factor design, no interaction analysis, and no combined clay-concentration × additive-dosage optimization. This is precisely the gap your Box-Behnken/RSM design closes, and it's worth stating explicitly as your key methodological improvement over this specific template paper in Chapter 3.

## Key findings

**XRF screening across all 7 sources (Table 1):** SiO₂ 48.6–53.5%, Al₂O₃ 16.2–21.4%, Fe₂O₃ 3.2–7.5%, Na₂O 3.5–4.2%, CaO 1.2–3.3%, K₂O 0.3–4.0%, LOI 14.0–20.5%. Authors note the Al₂O₃/SiO₂ ratio is roughly 1/3 or greater across all seven sources, consistent with Wyoming bentonite and satisfying the basic requirement expected in montmorillonite — a simple screening heuristic, not a mineralogical proof, worth noting as a lighter-weight diagnostic than full XRD.

**⚠️ Na/Ca classification — a discrepancy worth flagging against your other sources:** The paper states the mass ratio of Na/Ca for Jhelum and Jundola bentonite is **1.5 and 1.6** respectively, and (Na+K)/(Ca+Mg) around **2** for both, and on that basis classifies **both as "Sodium Bentonite"** (citing Falode et al. 2008 for the classification criterion). This is a **bulk-oxide-based Na/Ca ratio** (computed directly from the XRF Table 1 wt% oxide data), not an exchangeable-cation/CEC-based ratio. This is exactly the same methodological pitfall already flagged in your Alshameri (2013) and El-Mahllawy (2013) summaries: bulk Na₂O/CaO ratios do not necessarily reflect the actual exchangeable-cation status at the clay's exchange sites. Unlike El-Mahllawy's samples (where bulk Na₂O > CaO but XRD proved Ca-dominant interlayer chemistry), **this paper doesn't report any XRD basal-spacing or CEC data to independently verify the "Sodium Bentonite" classification** — it rests entirely on the bulk-oxide ratio. Given that this is your stated primary methodological template, and given that the paper's own rheological results below (very low viscosity/high filtrate loss for raw clay, needing heavy polymer dosing to reach API spec) look much more consistent with **Ca-dominant behavior** than with genuine Na-bentonite behavior, **treat the "Sodium Bentonite" classification in this paper with caution** — it may be another case of bulk-oxide Na/Ca ratio overstating true Na-exchange character, similar to the pattern already established across your other sources. Worth flagging explicitly in Chapter 2 as a case where a paper's own stated classification is questionable by the same logic you're using to critique your other sources' oxide-based ratios.

**Raw clay rheology at increasing concentration (Section 3.1, Figs. 3–5):** Both Jhelum and Jundola showed a strong, monotonic increase in apparent viscosity, plastic viscosity, yield point, and gel strength as clay concentration rose from 21 to 100 g/350 mL. **Optimum concentration approximating Mill Gel's rheological performance:** 80 g/350 mL for Jhelum, 60 g/350 mL for Jundola — i.e., **roughly 3–4× the standard 21 g/350 mL (6 wt%) API reference loading** was needed for the raw, unbeneficiated local clays to match commercial bentonite's rheology. This is a clean, directly citable "raw local clay is usable but inefficient at standard loading" data point — a milder version of the "raw material is deficient, needs treatment" narrative running through your whole reference base.

**Raw clay filtration (Section 3.2, Fig. 6):** Filtrate loss decreased as clay concentration increased for both bentonites, but the pattern diverged: **Jhelum's filtrate loss remained higher than Mill Gel's at every concentration tested** (never caught up even at 100 g/350 mL); **Jundola's filtrate loss dropped below Mill Gel's specifically at 100 g/350 mL.** Authors conclude that low bentonite concentrations require additive-based fluid-loss control — directly motivating the beneficiation trials that follow.

**Beneficiation with Xanthan gum (XG) alone (Section 3.3.1, Figs. 7–8):** At fixed 21 g/350 mL (6 wt%) clay concentration, XG dosed at 0.5–2.5 g. Headline result: adding **9.5% Xanthan gum** (interpret as XG mass relative to clay mass, i.e., ~2 g XG per 21 g clay) to Jhelum mud raised the dial reading at 600 rpm from 5 to 70 — a **13-fold increase**, with plastic viscosity increasing **6.5×** and yield point increasing **40×**. Jundola showed even larger rheological gains than Jhelum at the same concentrations. Gel strength (10-sec and 10-min) also trended upward with XG dose.

**Combined XG + CMC HV (Section 3.3.1 cont'd, Figs. 9–10):** Fixed 1 g XG + varying CMC HV (0.5–2.5 g). At **5% XG and 9.5% CMC HV** (again read as relative to clay mass) added to 21 g/350 mL Jhelum mud, dial reading at 600 rpm rose from 5 to 63 (**11.5-fold increase**), with plastic viscosity up **8×** and yield point up **31×** — meeting API rheological requirements. Jundola again outperformed Jhelum in plastic viscosity, yield point, and gel-strength formation under the same additive treatment.

**Filtration control — CMC HV + XG (Section 3.3.2, Fig. 11a):** Filtrate loss dropped sharply as CMC HV dose increased at fixed 1 g XG. At **7% CMC HV + 1 g XG**, Jhelum's filtrate loss reduced to **3.4× less than its original (untreated) value**. Jundola again outperformed Jhelum in filtration control at matched additive doses.

**Filtration control — Starch + XG (Section 3.3.2 cont'd, Fig. 11b):** At **12% starch + 1 g XG**, Jhelum's filtrate loss was reduced **3.7-fold**. Mud cake thickness dropped by **55% (starch)** and **33% (CMC HV)** relative to untreated mud — starch outperformed CMC HV specifically on cake-thickness/filtration control, while CMC HV gave the best combined viscosity + filtration improvement overall (per the abstract's own framing). This is a clean, three-way comparative conclusion directly worth citing: **XG → best rheology improvement; CMC HV → best combined viscosity + filtration control; starch → best filtration/cake-thickness control specifically.**

**Overall conclusion:** Both Jhelum and Jundola bentonites, when beneficiated with XG, CMC HV, and/or starch, met API specification (minimum 600-rpm dial reading of 30, maximum filtration loss of 15 cm³) and are proposed as viable substitutes for imported commercial bentonite in Pakistani drilling operations.

## Numbers worth citing directly

- Optimum raw-clay concentration to match Mill Gel rheology: Jhelum 80 g/350 mL; Jundola 60 g/350 mL (vs. 21 g/350 mL API standard reference loading)
- XG-only improvement (Jhelum, 21 g/350 mL): 9.5% XG → 13× increase in 600-rpm dial reading (5→70), 6.5× PV increase, 40× YP increase
- XG+CMC HV improvement (Jhelum, 21 g/350 mL): 5% XG + 9.5% CMC HV → 11.5× dial-reading increase (5→63), 8× PV increase, 31× YP increase
- Filtrate-loss reduction, CMC HV+XG (Jhelum): 7% CMC HV + 1g XG → 3.4× reduction
- Filtrate-loss reduction, starch+XG (Jhelum): 12% starch + 1g XG → 3.7× reduction
- Mud cake thickness reduction: 55% (starch), 33% (CMC HV)
- Stated Na/Ca mass ratios (bulk-oxide-based, flagged as questionable classification basis): Jhelum 1.5, Jundola 1.6; (Na+K)/(Ca+Mg) ≈ 2 for both
- API benchmark cited (13A/13 Seventeenth Ed., 2006 — note this is an older edition than your own 2010 18th-ed. source): minimum 600-rpm dial reading 30, maximum filtration loss 15 cm³

## Caveats / limitations

1. **One-factor-at-a-time additive dosing only** (single additive varied at fixed 21 g/350 mL clay concentration; no multi-factor design, no clay-concentration × additive-dosage interaction tested) — the clearest and most direct precedent yet for stating your Box-Behnken/RSM approach as a methodological improvement, since this is literally your named "primary methodological template."
2. **"Sodium Bentonite" classification for Jhelum/Jundola rests solely on a bulk-oxide Na/Ca mass ratio** (via XRF, citing Falode et al. 2008's classification criterion) with no accompanying XRD basal-spacing or CEC/exchangeable-cation data to confirm it — treat this classification with the same skepticism you've applied to bulk-oxide-derived Na/Ca claims elsewhere in your reference base; the raw rheological/filtration behavior described (needing 3–4× standard clay loading, or heavy polymer dosing, to reach commercial-bentonite performance) reads more consistent with Ca-dominant clay behavior than genuine Na-bentonite behavior.
3. Percentage additive doses (e.g., "9.5% Xanthan gum," "12% starch") are not explicitly defined in the text as a fraction of clay mass, mud mass, or water mass — inferred here as relative to clay mass based on context (dose ranges given in grams alongside these percentages), but **worth double-checking against the original Fig. 7–11 axis labels/units directly if you need to reproduce these dosing rates precisely** for your own comparative discussion.
4. API standard cited in this paper (13A/13, 17th ed., 2006) is an older edition than your own primary source (2010, 18th ed.) — the two editions may differ slightly in some parameter values; when directly comparing your API benchmark table against this paper's stated API spec, note the edition difference.
5. No XRD, DTA/TGA, or CEC data reported at all for Jhelum/Jundola specifically (only bulk XRF chemistry) — this is a comparatively "thin" characterization relative to your other core sources (Alshameri, Hassan, El-Mahllawy all include XRD basal spacing at minimum); don't over-rely on this paper for mineralogical rigor, only for the rheological/filtration benchmarking methodology and results.
6. Two-deposit study within one country — useful as a process/methodology template, not a geological analog to Alaslaf's Triassic rhyolitic-tuff origin.
