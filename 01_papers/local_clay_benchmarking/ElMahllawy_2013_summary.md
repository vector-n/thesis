# El-Mahllawy et al. (2013) — The Composition and Activation Aspects of El-Fayoum Clays for Using as a Drilling Fluid

**Full citation:** El-Mahllawy, M.S., Sharara, A.M., Hassaan, M.M., Abdel Haleem, A.M. (2013). "The composition and activation aspects of El-Fayoum clays for using as a drilling fluid." *Egyptian Journal of Petroleum*, 22, 395–404 (article-in-press pagination shown as pp. 1–10 in typeset proof). DOI: 10.1016/j.ejpe.2013.10.009. Received 20 Nov 2012; accepted 15 Jan 2013. Open access (Egyptian Petroleum Research Institute). Authors affiliated with Housing and Building National Research Center, Cairo, and Al-Azhar University, Cairo.

**Deposit/location:** Kom Oshim area, El-Fayoum province, Egypt — claystone quarries ~30 km north of El-Fayoum city (29°31′–29°35′ N, 30°50′–30°59′ E). Host unit: Qasr El-Sagha Formation (Upper Eocene). Five samples collected from five separate quarries, labeled q9, q10, q11, q35, q124.

**Method:** Full characterization (XRD, XRF, DTA/TGA, laser-diffraction grain size, Atterberg limits, free swell, activity ratio) of five raw claystone samples → rheological testing of slurries at incremental concentrations (4.5–7.5 wt%, non-activated) → activation trials using two separate activators (1.5 wt% caustic soda/NaOH, and 0.5 wt% POLYPAC-R polyanionic cellulose polymer) at fixed 6.5 wt% clay concentration → benchmarking against OCMA and API drilling-fluid specifications.

## Key findings

**Geological context (Chapter 1 background use):** Qasr El-Sagha Formation is a 5-unit Upper Eocene sequence; the lowest two units (~110 m combined) are the claystone-siltstone interval that hosts the bentonitic clay, locally exploited around Kom Oshim village. Formation thickness varies dramatically across the province — from ~180 m at Gebel Qasr El-Sagha to ~40 m at Qaret El-Faras — a useful cautionary example of how much lateral heterogeneity a single formation/deposit can show, relevant to your own Phase 0 concern about confirming exact sampling coordinates and representativeness at Alaslaf.

**Mineralogy (semi-quantitative, Table 1):**

| Sample | Montmorillonite % | Kaolinite % | Illite % |
|---|---|---|---|
| q9 | 65 | 13 | 4 |
| q10 | 66 | 14 | 2 |
| q11 | 63 | 12 | 4 |
| q35 | **75** (highest) | 11 | 2 |
| q124 | **47** (lowest) | 18 | 5 |

Quartz and calcite present as minor non-clay components in all samples. XRD basal spacing (001) at **14–15 Å in air-dried state**, corresponding to montmorillonite with predominantly Ca as interlayer cation — again closely matching the 14.45–15.31 Å range from Hassan & Abdel-Khalek (1998, Gabal Hamdal) and the 15.3 Å from Alshameri (2013, Alaslaf). **This is now a third independent deposit showing the same ~14–15.3 Å natural/Ca-dominant basal-spacing signature** — a genuinely strong cross-regional pattern worth stating explicitly in your Chapter 1/2 as supporting evidence that this basal-spacing range is a reliable field diagnostic for Ca-dominant smectite before you even run a full CEC/exchangeable-cation test. After heating to 550°C: montmorillonite peak collapses to 9.5 Å (standard dehydration response); after glycolation: expands to 17–18 Å (standard smectite-swelling confirmation) — useful as a methods reference for your own XRD protocol/expected diagnostic peak shifts.

**Chemical composition (Table 2, wt%):** All samples dominated by SiO₂ (45.6–51.3%), Al₂O₃ (17.3–27.0%), Fe₂O₃ (7.8–13.2%), with minor CaO (0.50–1.88%), MgO (1.60–2.08%), Na₂O (1.41–2.87%), K₂O (0.20–1.87%). **Notably, bulk Na₂O consistently exceeds bulk CaO by a wide margin here** (e.g., q9: Na₂O 1.41% vs CaO 0.50%) — this is a good illustration of the exact methodological trap flagged in your Alshameri (2013) summary: despite bulk Na₂O > CaO, the XRD data clearly shows these are Ca-dominant smectites in their actual exchange sites (14–15 Å basal spacing, described explicitly by the authors as "predominantly Ca as interlayer cation"). **Bulk oxide Na₂O/CaO ratios are once again shown to be an unreliable proxy for exchangeable Na/Ca status** — this paper is a clean, citable illustration of that exact pitfall, useful to strengthen the methodological caveat you're building across your reference base.

pH: alkaline range, 8.35 (q9) to 10.17 (q124). LOI: 11.22–13.83%.

**Grain size / physical properties (Tables 3–4):** Clay fraction (<2 µm) ranged 70.7% (q124) to 89.6% (q35). Atterberg limits: liquid limit 74 (q124) to 185 (q10); free swell 140% (q124) to 600% (q10). Activity ratio: q9/q10/q11 = "active" class, q35 = "normal" class, **q124 = "inactive" class (activity ratio 0.60)** — q124 is consistently the worst-performing sample across every single test in this paper, always tracking its lowest montmorillonite content (47%) and highest non-swelling silt fraction (29.3%).

**Rheology before activation (Tables 5–7):** All five raw samples tested at 4.5%, 5.5%, 6.5%, 7.5% clay concentration. Viscosity, yield point, and gel strength all increased with concentration; filtrate loss decreased with concentration. **None of the raw/non-activated samples met both OCMA and API specifications simultaneously** — q11 and q35 came closest, meeting the OCMA filtrate limit (≤16 mL) only at the higher 6.5–7.5% concentrations, but even they did not fully satisfy the API spec across all parameters.

**OCMA/API benchmark specs used (Table 8) — note a discrepancy worth flagging:**

| Parameter | API | OCMA |
|---|---|---|
| Dial reading @600rpm | min 30 | min 30 |
| YP/PV ratio | max 3.0 | max 6.0 |
| Filtrate loss | **max 13.0 mL** | max 16.0 mL |

**Compare this to Magzoub (2014)'s stated API filtrate maximum of 15 cm³ and OCMA maximum of 12.5 cm³** — the two secondary sources you now have on file state *different* numeric filtrate limits for the same nominal standards. This is worth resolving before you finalize your own Chapter 3 benchmark table: **go to the primary API 13A/13B and OCMA standard documents directly** (you already have the 2010 API 13A 18th ed. and 2024 API 13B-1 ballot draft in your own reference base) rather than propagating secondhand numbers from other theses/papers, since at least one of these two secondary citations must be either using a different edition or transcribing imprecisely.

**Activation results (Table 9, Section 4.3) — the paper's key finding:**
- Both 1.5 wt% NaOH and 0.5 wt% POLYPAC-R (tested separately, at fixed 6.5 wt% clay concentration) significantly improved rheological properties.
- **4 of 5 samples (q9, q10, q11, q35) successfully met both OCMA and API specifications after activation.**
- **q124 remained non-compliant even after activation** — attributed explicitly to its low montmorillonite content (47%) and high non-swelling silt fraction; authors note it is "inactive" per its activity ratio (0.60) and would need either higher activator concentration or a different activator type.
- **POLYPAC-R outperformed NaOH** across the board in improving rheological properties — a directly useful comparative data point if you're considering polymer vs. inorganic-salt activation strategies for Alaslaf.
- Activated plastic viscosity ranged **7 cP (q11 with NaOH, q35 with POLYPAC-R) to 10 cP (q9 with NaOH, q124 with POLYPAC-R)** — again benchmarked against the same Meycogel (5.0 cP) / Wyoming (~11.0 cP) reference pair used in Hassan & Abdel-Khalek (1998), suggesting this Meycogel/Wyoming PV benchmark pair is a standard reference point circulating through the Egyptian bentonite literature — worth adopting as a citable comparator in your own results chapter.
- Reaction mechanism stated explicitly: Ca-bentonite + NaOH → Na-bentonite + Ca(OH)₂ — a clean, simple, directly citable mechanistic statement for your Chapter 2/3 background on why NaOH (or Na₂CO₃) activation works.
