**Type of paper:** Pure RSM/Box-Behnken **methodology** paper — no local-clay deposit or beneficiation content, but genuinely bentonite-containing (as part of a saltwater base mud) and, importantly, **the closest response-set match to your own thesis of any paper in your reference base**: it tracks **7 responses** — viscosity (R1), plastic viscosity (R2), yield point (R3), 10-second gel strength (R4), 10-minute gel strength (R5), filtrate loss (R6), and mud cake thickness (R7) — a superset that covers essentially all 5 of your own planned responses (PV, YP, AV/viscosity, gel strength, fluid loss) plus mud cake thickness as a bonus.

## Method

**3-factor Box-Behnken design, Design-Expert software.** Factors: Carboxymethyl Cellulose (CMC, X₁, 1–5 g), a proprietary **"alkaline polymer"** (X₂, 1–5 g — described in the text as an adhesive/construction-grade polymer, colloquially "brick glue," proposed as a **single multifunctional substitute** for PAC-LV, PAC-R, KOH, and NaOH simultaneously), and xanthan gum (X₃, 0–2 g). Base mud: saltwater with dissolved salts, bentonite dispersed and pre-hydrated first, then CMC/alkaline polymer/xanthan gum added under standardized high-speed mixing. **Multi-temperature framework**: samples thermally conditioned and tested at 80°F (near-surface), 150°F (typical intermediate downhole), and 250°F (HTHP/deep or geothermal), explicitly intended to simulate progressive downhole thermal exposure — a design dimension none of your other RSM sources include, and one your own thesis (room-temperature only, per current proposal) does not currently plan to test.

**Rheological measurement:** standard Fann rotational viscometer, dial readings at 600/300/200/100/6/3 rpm — same measurement basis as your own plan. Filtrate loss and mud cake thickness measured via standard filter press at 80°F for all formulations (i.e. fluid-loss testing itself was not repeated at 150/250°F). Density (~8.65 ppg) and pH (near-neutral) recorded for completeness but not treated as optimization responses.

**Multi-response optimization:** Design-Expert's desirability-function module used to **maximize viscosity, PV, YP, and gel strength while simultaneously minimizing filtrate loss and mud cake thickness** — this is explicitly a **6-objective, opposing-direction simultaneous desirability optimization**, the broadest multi-response framing of any paper in your reference base (broader even than Asmungi 2021's 3-response case). However, note the caveats below on how completely this is actually reported.

## Key results

**ANOVA summary (Table 2)** — note: only a single ANOVA table is given, not one per temperature level, despite the stated three-temperature framework (see caveats):

| Response | Model F | p-value | R² | Adj. R² |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Viscosity (R1) | 32.45 | <0.001 | 0.97 | 0.95 |
| Plastic Viscosity (R2) | 21.88 | <0.001 | 0.94 | 0.91 |
| Yield Point (R3) | 18.73 | <0.005 | 0.92 | 0.88 |
| Gel Strength 10s (R4) | 14.56 | <0.010 | 0.90 | 0.87 |
| Gel Strength 10min (R5) | 16.32 | <0.010 | 0.91 | 0.88 |
| Filtrate Loss (R6) | 7.82 | <0.050 | 0.87 | 0.82 |
| Mud Cake (R7) | 3.21 | >0.050 (non-significant) | 0.65 | 0.58 |

**Mud cake thickness (R7) is the one response that fails to reach a statistically significant quadratic model** (p>0.05, R²=0.65) — the paper's own interpretation is that mud cake thickness "is influenced more strongly by inherent material properties rather than the interactions modeled within the RSM framework." **This is a directly useful precedent for your own thesis**: if your own fluid-loss/mud-cake response similarly resists a clean quadratic fit, you now have a second citable example (alongside Alhajabdalla 2021's zero-variance XG/3mm case) of a response that a well-designed BBD simply doesn't model well, without that being a flaw in your experimental execution.

**Reported optimum (150°F only): CMC = 3.5 g, alkaline polymer = 3.6 g** → predicted viscosity = 17.64 cP, PV = 12.46 cP, YP = 7.72 lb/100ft². 3D surface (80°F) and contour plot (150°F) both show the optimum/peak region in essentially the **same compositional location** (≈3.3–3.6 g CMC, ≈3.4–3.6 g alkaline polymer) — i.e. **temperature appears to change the magnitude of the response surface but not the location of the optimum**, an interesting (if under-discussed) observation worth flagging if you ever compare against your own single-temperature results.

**Directional/mechanistic findings:** both CMC and alkaline polymer increase viscosity, PV, YP, and gel strength (synergistic/additive, attributed to CMC's hydration/swelling plus the alkaline polymer's adhesive network-reinforcement), with diminishing returns (curvature) at high concentrations of either. Viscosity and PV respond strongly to **both** additives combined; YP and gel strength are described as depending **more strongly on the alkaline polymer than on CMC**, attributed to the alkaline polymer's superior thermal stability and bridging/adhesive capability at elevated temperature.

## Relevance to this thesis

- **Chapter 3 — your best available response-set match.** No other paper in your reference base tracks a response list this close to your own five (PV, YP, AV, gel strength, fluid loss); useful as a structural template for how to report ANOVA across many responses compactly (their Table 2 format — one row per response with F/p/R²/adj.R² — is a clean, citable model for your own results tables).
- **Chapter 3/5 — non-significant-model precedent for mud cake/fluid loss.** If your own BBD produces a weak or non-significant model for gel strength or fluid loss, you now have two independent citable precedents (this paper's mud cake result, Alhajabdalla's zero-variance case) that this is a known, unremarkable occurrence in BBD-based drilling-fluid optimization, not necessarily a flaw in your design or execution.
- **Chapter 3 — multi-response desirability precedent, but a qualified one for Open Thread #4.** The stated methodology (6 simultaneous opposing objectives via Design-Expert desirability) is exactly the kind of genuine multi-response worked example you were looking for — more ambitious in scope than Asmungi (2021)'s 3-response case. **However**, treat this cautiously as a "confirmed" precedent (see caveats): the actual reported optimum only states 3 of the 7 response values (viscosity, PV, YP) and never states the corresponding optimized filtrate loss, mud cake thickness, or gel strength values, nor the optimal xanthan gum dosage. The desirability-based *approach* is citable; the specific *outcome* is incompletely reported.
- **Chapter 1/2 — "multifunctional additive replacing several conventional chemicals" framing.** The alkaline-polymer-as-substitute-for-PAC-LV/PAC-R/KOH/NaOH narrative is a nice parallel to your own thesis's implicit argument (activated local Yemeni bentonite + CMC replacing imported Wyoming bentonite/Mill Gel systems) — both are "simplify and localize the additive package" arguments, useful supporting citation if you want a second contemporary example beyond your own case.
- **Not useful for mineralogical/local-deposit content** — bentonite here is an unspecified-dosage background component of a generic saltwater base mud, not a characterized local clay; keep this firmly in your Chapter 3 methodology bucket only.

## Caveats / limitations

1. **No fitted regression equations given for any of the 7 responses.** Unlike Asmungi (2021), Alhajabdalla (2021), Afolabi (2018), and Ossai (2025), this paper only presents the *general* coded quadratic form (Eq. 1, with symbolic β coefficients) and never reports the actual fitted numeric coefficients for any response. You cannot extract specific per-factor effect magnitudes from this paper the way you can from your other RSM sources — only the qualitative directional narrative and the summary ANOVA table.
2. **No per-term (individual factor/interaction) p-values reported** — Table 2 gives only overall model-level F/p/R²/adj.R², not a term-by-term breakdown (unlike Alhajabdalla's Table 7). Claims like "YP depends more on alkaline polymer than CMC" are supported only by qualitative interpretation of 3D surface shapes in the text, not by a citable numeric coefficient or p-value.
3. **Missing 250°F-specific results.** The abstract and methodology explicitly frame this as a three-temperature (80/150/250°F) study, but the Results section as available only substantively presents 80°F (viscosity 3D surface) and 150°F (contour plot, final reported optimum) — no explicit 250°F-specific model, figure, or optimum formulation appears in the text. The HTHP/250°F framing in the title and abstract is not fully supported by the results actually shown; don't cite this paper as demonstrating a validated 250°F optimum without checking the full published PDF/supplementary material for a 250°F table you may have missed.
4. **Design-matrix irregularity.** Table 1 (composition matrix) lists **17 runs**, not the standard 15 for a 3-factor Box-Behnken design (12 edge-midpoints + 3 centre-point replicates); the centre point (CMC=3, alkaline polymer=3, xanthan=1) appears to repeat **5 times** rather than the usual 3. This may reflect genuine additional replication, a multi-temperature reuse of the same formulations, or a transcription/reporting inconsistency — the text doesn't clarify. Note this if you use the paper as a structural BBD template; don't assume it's a clean textbook 15-run design the way Alhajabdalla's and Asmungi's are.
5. **Xanthan gum's role is incompletely resolved.** It's named as the third DOE factor (0–2 g) and appears in the composition table, but the final reported "optimal formulation" (150°F) states only CMC and alkaline polymer values — no optimal xanthan gum dosage is given. Effectively, only 2 of the 3 stated factors are shown varying in the 3D/contour plots (Figs. 2, 3, 5 all plot CMC vs. alkaline polymer only). Treat this as an effectively 2-factor result set despite the nominal 3-factor design, unless you can confirm the missing xanthan value from the full paper.
6. **Bentonite dosage is never quantified.** Bentonite is present in the base mud ("dispersed in the base fluid and pre-hydrated") but no gram quantity, source, or supplier is given anywhere in the visible text — unlike Asmungi (2021), where bentonite was itself a controlled DOE factor with a stated range. Don't cite this paper as a bentonite-dosage precedent.
7. **"Alkaline polymer" is an unspecified proprietary/generic product** ("also often used to glue bricks," i.e., a construction-adhesive-type polymer) — no chemical identity, supplier, or specification is disclosed. Treat as a black-box commercial additive reference only; it cannot be cited for any chemistry-specific claim.
8. **Small validation dataset.** The predicted-vs-actual plot (Fig. 4) appears to show only about 5 data points total for all responses combined — a very small basis for the paper's claim of "close clustering" and "high fidelity" model validation. Cite the R²/adj.R² values from Table 2 rather than leaning on the qualitative Fig. 4 discussion.
9. **Heavy self-citation cluster.** Several references (Maulani et al. 2024, Samura et al. 2024, Yanti et al. 2024, Rahanjani & Nugraha 2025, Sismartono et al. 2023) are by overlapping author teams from the same Universitas Trisakti group as this paper. Not a flaw in itself, but — similar to your Magzoub 2014/2018 caution — don't treat multiple citations from this cluster as fully independent corroboration if you ever cite more than one of them together.
10. Indonesian laboratory study, no Nigerian/Saudi/Egyptian/Yemeni geological or deposit context — keep firmly in your Chapter 3 methodology citation group, separate from your regional local-clay-benchmarking literature.
