# Alhajabdalla, Mahmoud, Nasser, Hussein, Ahmed & Karami (2021) — Application of RSM and Box-Behnken Design for the Optimization of the Stability of Fibrous Dispersion Used in Drilling and Completion Operations

**Full citation:** Alhajabdalla, M., Mahmoud, H., Nasser, M.S., Hussein, I.A., Ahmed, R., Karami, H. (2021). "Application of Response Surface Methodology and Box–Behnken Design for the Optimization of the Stability of Fibrous Dispersion Used in Drilling and Completion Operations." *ACS Omega*, 6, 2513–2525. DOI: 10.1021/acsomega.0c04272. Open access (CC-BY-NC-ND). Authors affiliated with Qatar University (Gas Processing Center) and University of Oklahoma (Petroleum and Geological Engineering). Received 1 Sep 2020, accepted 4 Jan 2021.

**Type of paper:** Not a bentonite/clay characterization or beneficiation study — this is a **pure RSM/Box-Behnken methodology paper** investigating fiber-suspension stability in three different water-based polymer systems (CMC, PAM, XG), with no clay component at all. Its value to your thesis is entirely methodological: it is your **most directly relevant worked example of RSM/BBD + desirability-function optimization actually implemented in Minitab for a drilling-fluid-adjacent system**, and it explicitly cites Derringer & Suich (1980) — already in your reference base — as its optimization-method source (ref [27] in this paper).

**Core problem:** fibers are added to water-based drilling/completion fluids to improve hole-cleaning and reduce fluid loss, but tend to segregate (migrate to the top of the fluid column) unless suspended by a sufficiently viscous/structuring base polymer. The study quantifies how base-fluid polymer concentration, fiber concentration, fiber aspect ratio, and temperature jointly determine suspension stability, using RSM/BBD across three separate polymer systems.

## Method

**Three base polymers tested independently (Table 1):** carboxymethyl cellulose sodium (CMC), non-ionic polyacrylamide (PAM), and xanthan gum (XG) — **CMC is directly relevant since it is one of your own thesis's three planned formulation factors** (clay concentration, Na₂CO₃ dosage, CMC concentration).

**Fibers:** virgin polypropylene monofilament, SG 0.91, melting point 163–177°C, two aspect ratios tested (3 mm and 12.5 mm length, both 100 μm diameter).

**3-factor Box-Behnken design, 15 runs per fiber length (Table 5)** — genuinely the **same classic 3-factor BBD structure your thesis will use** (12 edge-midpoints + 3 center-point replicates), unlike the 4-factor CCD/BBD designs seen in your Afolabi (2018) and Ossai (2025) summaries. **Factor levels (Table 2):**

| Factor | Low (−1) | Central (0) | High (+1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base fluid polymer concentration (vol%) | 1 | 4.5 | 8 |
| Fiber concentration (wt%) | 0.01 | 0.045 | 0.08 |
| Temperature (°C) | 25 | 52.5 | 80 |

90 total experimental trials (3 polymers × 2 fiber lengths × 15 BBD runs), randomized to minimize bias.

**Response variable:** suspension stability $S_S$ (%), defined via Eq. 1 as $S_S = (BV-RBV)/(TV-RBV)$, where BV = measured fiber bed volume in the polymeric suspension after a 1-hour quiescent settling period in an oven at test temperature, RBV = reference bed volume in pure water (baseline), TV = total suspension volume. **0% = same as pure water (fully unstable), 100% = fully stable (zero fiber bed volume, i.e. fibers remain fully dispersed).** This dispersion/settling-based stability metric is conceptually analogous to (though methodologically distinct from) sag/settling tests sometimes used for barite sag in weighted drilling fluids — a useful methodological parallel if your thesis discusses particle settling/suspension stability at all, though your own responses (PV/YP/AV/gel strength/fluid loss) don't include a direct settling-stability metric.

**Statistical model:** standard second-order polynomial (Eq. 2), same general form as all your other RSM sources: $Y = n_0 + n_1X_A + n_2X_B + n_3X_C + n_4X_A^2 + n_5X_B^2 + n_6X_C^2 + n_7X_AX_B + n_8X_AX_C + n_9X_BX_C$. Regression and ANOVA performed in **Minitab** — matching your own planned software.

## Key results

**Fitted models and R² by system (12.5 mm fibers):** CMC R²=0.99, PAM R²=0.95, XG R²=0.91 (Table/text, Section 3.1). **3 mm fibers:** CMC R²=0.99, PAM R²=0.99; **XG regression could not be established for 3 mm fibers because all 15 runs showed complete (100%) stability regardless of factor levels** — a genuinely interesting null result (a response with zero variance cannot be regressed) worth noting as a real-world example of when RSM modeling simply isn't applicable to a given response.

**Dominant factor across all systems: base fluid polymer concentration.** In every one of the five successfully modeled systems (CMC-12.5mm, PAM-12.5mm, XG-12.5mm, CMC-3mm, PAM-3mm), the polymer concentration term $X_A$ and its quadratic $X_A^2$ are the most statistically significant terms (p<0.05, often p<0.001), while fiber concentration ($X_B$) and temperature ($X_C$) are **not** individually significant in most models (e.g., CMC-12.5mm: $X_B$ p=0.123, $X_C$ p=0.909; Table 7). This is a clean, strongly stated finding: **polymer/viscosifier concentration dominates system behavior far more than the secondary additive (fiber) concentration** — directly parallel to the polymer-dominance finding already flagged in your Afolabi (2018) summary (Kelzan XCD polymer coefficient 3–4× larger than bentonite content across all four rheology responses).

**Interaction/mechanistic findings (useful qualitative framing material):**
- At **low** polymer concentration, temperature effects on stability become significant (destabilizing at higher temp, since viscosity drops); at **high** polymer concentration, temperature and fiber-concentration effects become negligible — "polymer concentration overwhelms other factors" is the paper's own framing (Section 3.3).
- **Fiber aspect ratio matters, but indirectly:** 12.5 mm (long) fibers show better stability at high temperature than 3 mm (short) fibers, because longer fibers entangle into a structured network that resists buoyant migration; short fibers don't network and instead migrate individually to the unstable top layer. This "long fiber = network = stability; short fiber = no network = instability" mechanism is directly analogous in structure (though not substance) to how your thesis frames Na-activation increasing basal spacing/structure in clay platelets — both are "structuring mechanism confers macroscopic stability" arguments, potentially useful as a cross-disciplinary analogy if you want one in your discussion chapter.
- Rheological measurements (separate from the BBD stability tests, Figure 6) showed **XG has by far the highest suspension viscosity** of the three polymers at all concentrations, and its viscosity is **insensitive to temperature below 50°C** (unlike CMC and PAM, both of which show strong temperature-driven viscosity loss) — XG is flagged as preferable for high-temperature applications specifically because of this thermal stability, though its high viscosity is noted as a practical limitation for wellbore hydraulics at high concentrations.

**Model validation (Section 3.2, Table 9–10):** four independent confirmation points (not part of the original 15-run design) tested against model predictions; **all points showed <20% error**, with error increasing as conditions approached the lower (−1) factor bounds — i.e., **model reliability is best near the design center/high end and weakest at the design's lower extremes**, a useful general RSM caveat to carry into your own thesis's discussion of prediction reliability across your design space.

**Optimization via Derringer & Suich desirability function (Section 3.4, Tables 11–12):** the paper explicitly uses the same desirability-function framework already summarized in your Derringer & Suich (1980) entry, targeting 100% stability at both ambient (25°C) and elevated (80°C) temperature. **All optimized desirability values were d > 0.95**, confirming the practical usability of the desirability approach even when optimizing toward a target (two-sided "target-is-best" desirability, matching the two-sided transformation type in Derringer & Suich) rather than simple maximization. This is a **directly citable real-world confirmation that the Derringer & Suich desirability framework works well in an actual Minitab-based drilling-fluid-adjacent RSM study** — useful bridging citation connecting your foundational Derringer & Suich (1980) summary to an applied precedent.

## Numbers worth citing directly

- 3-factor BBD, 15 runs (12 edge-midpoint + 3 center-point replicates) — structurally identical to your own planned design
- Factor ranges: polymer 1–8 vol%, fiber 0.01–0.08 wt%, temperature 25–80°C
- Model R² values: CMC 0.99 (both fiber lengths), PAM 0.95 (12.5mm)/0.99 (3mm), XG 0.91 (12.5mm only; 3mm XG showed zero variance — total stability at all conditions, model inapplicable)
- All optimized desirability values d > 0.95 for 100%-stability target across both ambient and elevated temperature scenarios
- Model validation: all 4 confirmation points showed <20% prediction error; error increased toward lower (−1) factor bounds
- Polymer concentration is the single dominant factor across every successfully modeled system; fiber concentration and temperature are frequently non-significant individually (p>0.05)

## Caveats / limitations

1. **No clay or bentonite involved at all** — do not cite this as a local-clay-benchmarking or beneficiation precedent; it belongs exclusively in your methodology/RSM-technique citation group, not alongside Falode/Khan/Hassan/etc.
2. **Response variable (fiber suspension stability, a settling/sedimentation-based metric) is conceptually different from your own planned rheological responses (PV, YP, AV, gel strength, fluid loss)** — useful as a design/statistics template only, not as a source of directly comparable numeric benchmarks.
3. The 3 mm/XG combination's "zero variance, model not applicable" result (Section 3.1/4) is a useful cautionary example, but also means this paper effectively only successfully models 5 of its 6 possible system combinations — worth being precise that not every well-designed BBD experiment yields a usable quadratic model for every response, if you want to cite this as a general RSM caveat.
4. Desirability optimization here targets a single response at a time within each polymer system (stability), rather than jointly optimizing multiple simultaneous responses via the geometric-mean composite desirability described in Derringer & Suich (1980) — i.e., this paper doesn't actually demonstrate the **multi**-response composite-D optimization your own thesis will need (five simultaneous responses); it only demonstrates single-response target optimization repeated across different temperature scenarios. You'll need to rely on Derringer & Suich (1980) itself, plus your own Ossai (2025) and Afolabi (2018) sources, for actual multi-response desirability precedent, if any of them provide it — worth checking Asmungi (2021) and Satiyawira (2025), still pending, for a genuine multi-response composite-desirability drilling-fluid example.
5. Qatar/Oklahoma-based, purely laboratory synthetic-fiber/polymer system study — no Nigerian, Saudi, Egyptian, or Yemeni geological context whatsoever; keep this firmly in the "methodology" citation bucket in your Chapter 3, separate from your regional comparator literature in Chapter 1/2.
