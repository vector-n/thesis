# Bahranowski, Klimek, Gaweł & Serwicka (2021) — Rehydration Driven Na-Activation of Bentonite: Evolution of the Clay Structure and Composition

**Full citation:** Bahranowski, K., Klimek, A., Gaweł, A., Serwicka, E.M. (2021). "Rehydration Driven Na-Activation of Bentonite—Evolution of the Clay Structure and Composition." *Materials*, 14, 7622. DOI: 10.3390/ma14247622. Open access (CC-BY). Authors affiliated with AGH University of Science and Technology and the Polish Academy of Sciences, Kraków, Poland. Received 1 Nov 2021, published 10 Dec 2021.

**Type of paper:** Pure Na-activation **mechanism** paper — no drilling-fluid rheological testing at all (no viscosity, gel strength, or filtration loss measurements anywhere in this paper). Its value to your thesis is entirely mechanistic/structural: it uses XRD and FTIR to track, in fine time-resolved detail, **exactly what happens inside the clay structure during Na-activation** — the process your own Phase 1 go/no-go decision depends on — and, unusually, expresses activator dosage in **CEC-equivalent units** rather than wt%, giving you a portable framework for thinking about dosage relative to your own Alaslaf clay's measured CEC once you have it.

---

## Method

**Novel two-step activation method** (the paper's central methodological contribution): (1) **thermal dehydration** of raw Ca-rich bentonite at 200°C for 3h to strip interlayer water from the montmorillonite (temperature chosen from the clay's own TG/DTG/DTA profile — a strong low-temperature endotherm at 125°C with a shoulder at 187°C, attributed to interlayer water release, with the double-peak shape itself diagnostic of Ca²⁺-dominated interlayer; 200°C removes essentially all interlayer water without triggering dehydroxylation, which only begins at 659°C); followed immediately by (2) **impregnation of the still-hot dehydrated clay with an aqueous Na₂CO₃ solution**, which simultaneously rehydrates the collapsed interlayer *and* delivers Na⁺ for the cation-exchange reaction. Water volume was calibrated (35 wt% of the parent clay) to be the **maximum amount that still leaves the product in a macroscopically dry state** — deliberately eliminating the drying step that most conventional Na-activation procedures require (see the paper's own literature comparison table, discussed below).

**Material:** commercial Kopernica bentonite (Slovakia), ~80% Ca-montmorillonite, CEC = 80 meq/100g (BaCl₂ replacement method, AAS analysis), minor biotite/quartz/feldspar. **Four Na₂CO₃ dosages, expressed as fractions of the clay's own CEC: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 CEC** (corresponding here to 2.1, 4.2, 6.3, and 8.4 g Na₂CO₃ per 100g of this specific clay — i.e., roughly 2.1%, 4.2%, 6.3%, and 8.4% by mass, given this clay's particular CEC). Structural evolution tracked by XRD and FTIR **immediately after activation and after 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks of ambient storage** — i.e., this paper studies natural aging kinetics rather than imposing a fixed curing period the way Karagüzel (2010) does (month-long sunlight curing) or your own protocol does (12h/8h soak).

## Key results

**XRD tracking of Ca→Na transformation (Section 3.2.1, Fig. 3):** the parent clay's basal (001) reflection sits at d=14.9 Å (Ca-form, double water layer in the interlayer). Na-rich montmorillonite shows a distinct, lower d-spacing of ~12.6–12.7 Å (single water layer). At the **lowest dosage tested (0.5 CEC)**, a shoulder at 12.7 Å appears immediately, indicating partial exchange, but **even after 4 weeks of ageing the 14.9 Å (Ca-form) peak remains dominant** — i.e., **0.5 CEC is insufficient to achieve full transformation to Na-rich montmorillonite within the observed timeframe**. At **1.0 CEC**, the material starts Ca-dominant but the Na-form peak becomes dominant after ~2 weeks of ageing, continuing to strengthen through 4 weeks. At **1.5 CEC**, exchange is faster still — a single, symmetric 12.6 Å peak (essentially complete Na-transformation) appears already after 2 weeks. At **2.0 CEC**, transformation is **essentially instantaneous** — the freshly-activated sample already shows only the Na-form peak, with no further change on ageing.

**⚠️ Central, directly actionable finding: "transformation to Na-rich montmorillonite required Na₂CO₃ concentration of at least 1.0 CEC" within the 4-week observation window** (the paper's own abstract wording). Below that threshold (0.5 CEC), the clay remained predominantly Ca-form even after a month.

**Byproduct mineralogy depends systematically on dosage (combined XRD + FTIR, Section 3.2.2–3.2.3):**
- **0.5 and 1.0 CEC:** displaced Ca²⁺ precipitates as **poorly crystalline calcite** (XRD (112) reflection ~3.03 Å, crystal size 60–100 nm via Scherrer equation; FTIR 1420 cm⁻¹ ν₃ carbonate band), coexisting with some **amorphous CaCO₃** (FTIR 1490 cm⁻¹ shoulder).
- **1.5 CEC:** no calcite detected at all by XRD — attributed to the **higher basicity of the more concentrated soda solution suppressing calcite crystallization**, so displaced Ca²⁺ instead stays as amorphous CaCO₃ only (FTIR 1490/1425 cm⁻¹ doublet of near-equal intensity).
- **2.0 CEC:** since only half the introduced Na₂CO₃ is consumed by exchange (assuming complete exchange at 1.0-CEC-equivalent), the **excess soda reacts with the displaced Ca²⁺ to form gaylussite** (Na₂Ca(CO₃)₂·5H₂O, a double Na-Ca carbonate — FTIR bands at 1415 and 2960 cm⁻¹), plus **trace portlandite, Ca(OH)₂** (XRD, appearing only after 3–4 weeks of ageing), reflecting the strongly basic conditions at this highest soda dosage.

**Mechanistic interpretation of the exchange kinetics:** near-surface Na⁺-for-Ca²⁺ exchange at crystallite edges is faster than Na⁺ diffusion into particle interiors / Ca²⁺ diffusion out — so in the early stages of activation, particle edges are Na-enriched while cores remain Ca-dominated; ageing progressively homogenizes this gradient. Higher soda concentration accelerates this homogenization dramatically (weeks → nearly instantaneous), directly explaining why dosage and ageing time trade off against each other.

## Numbers worth citing directly

- Parent clay CEC = 80 meq/100g (BaCl₂ method); untreated d₀₀₁ = 14.9 Å (Ca-form, double water layer)
- Na-rich montmorillonite signature: d₀₀₁ ≈ 12.6–12.7 Å (single water layer)
- **At least 1.0 CEC-equivalent Na₂CO₃ dosage required for genuine transformation to Na-rich montmorillonite within 4 weeks**; 0.5 CEC insufficient even after 4 weeks
- Dosage-dependent kinetics: 1.0 CEC → dominant Na-form after ~2 weeks; 1.5 CEC → essentially complete after 2 weeks; 2.0 CEC → essentially instantaneous
- Thermal pretreatment: 200°C, 3h, chosen to remove interlayer water without initiating dehydroxylation (which begins at 659°C per this clay's own TG/DTA)
- Water content for dry-impregnation: 35 wt% of parent clay (empirically determined maximum keeping the product macroscopically dry)
- Byproduct mineralogy shifts with dosage: calcite+amorphous CaCO₃ (0.5–1.0 CEC) → amorphous CaCO₃ only (1.5 CEC) → gaylussite + trace portlandite (2.0 CEC)

## Caveats / limitations

1. **No rheological or drilling-fluid property testing whatsoever** — no viscosity, gel strength, or filtration loss data anywhere in this paper. It confirms *structural* Na-for-Ca transformation via XRD/FTIR, not that this transformation actually delivers API-standard drilling-mud performance. You'll need to pair this paper's structural/mechanistic findings with your rheological-benchmarking sources (Khan 2017, Karagüzel 2010, Magzoub papers) to close that loop.
2. **Dosage is clay-specific in absolute terms.** The "≥1.0 CEC needed" finding is expressed relative to *this* clay's CEC (80 meq/100g) — the equivalent wt% threshold will be different for Alaslaf bentonite, which will almost certainly have a different CEC. Don't cite "4.2%" as a universal number; cite the **CEC-equivalent framing** (≥1.0 CEC) as the transferable finding, and recompute the wt% once your own clay's CEC is measured.
3. **Different activation chemistry than plain wet-slurry methods.** This paper's specific secondary-phase mineralogy findings (calcite → amorphous CaCO₃ → gaylussite/portlandite, as dosage increases) are tied to its particular thermal-pretreatment + dry-impregnation method. Don't assume identical secondary-phase behavior will occur with a conventional wet-slurry Na-activation protocol (like your own or Karagüzel's) without independent verification.
4. **Single deposit, single set of experiments, no replication or statistical treatment** — XRD/FTIR peak assignments and dosage thresholds are based on visual/qualitative pattern interpretation across one clay, not a replicated or statistically validated study. Treat the "1.0 CEC threshold" as a well-reasoned single-study finding, not a universal law.
5. **Ageing tracked only to 4 weeks.** No data on longer-term (months/years) stability or further compositional evolution of the activated product beyond that window.
6. **Dosage range tested (0.5–2.0 CEC) is bounded** — the paper doesn't test below 0.5 or above 2.0 CEC, so conclusions shouldn't be extrapolated outside that range.
7. Slovak commercial deposit, no Yemen/Middle-East geological connection — keep this firmly in your `na_activation_mechanism` citation group alongside Karagüzel (2010), used for mechanism/protocol-design reasoning rather than as a regional comparator.
