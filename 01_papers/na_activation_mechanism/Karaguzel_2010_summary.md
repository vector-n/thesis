# Karagüzel, Çetinel, Boylu, Çinku & Çelik (2010) — Activation of (Na, Ca)-bentonites with Soda and MgO and Their Utilization as Drilling Mud

**Full citation:** Karagüzel, C., Çetinel, T., Boylu, F., Çinku, K., Çelik, M.S. (2010). "Activation of (Na, Ca)-bentonites with soda and MgO and their utilization as drilling mud." *Applied Clay Science*, 48, 398–404. DOI: 10.1016/j.clay.2010.01.013. Authors affiliated with Dumlupınar University, Istanbul Technical University, and Istanbul University, Turkey. Received 16 Jul 2009, accepted 24 Jan 2010.

## Materials and deposit context

**Two Turkish (Na, Ca)-bentonites**, CGB (gray) and CYB (yellow), mined from Çanbensan Bentonite Company, Çankırı province, Turkey. XRF chemistry (Table 2): Na₂O 2.3% (CGB) / 2.5% (CYB), MgO 1.9%/2.5%, CaO 3.0%/2.1%, Al₂O₃ 17.3%/15.6%, SiO₂ 61.9%/57.8%, LOI 7.9%/12.5%. **Mass ratio (Na₂O+K₂O)/(CaO+MgO) = 0.57**, which the authors classify (per Günay et al., 2001) as placing both samples in the **"mixed bentonite"** category — a classification scheme conceptually parallel to (though not identical in formula to) the Na/Ca and (Na+K)/(Ca+Mg) ratio thresholds you're already using from Khan (2017) for your own Alaslaf go/no-go decision. Worth cross-checking whether Günay et al.'s classification bands and Khan's Na/Ca<0.5 threshold are compatible or divergent if you want to cite both systems together.

**XRD mineralogy (Fig. 1, quantitative modal analysis per Chung 1974/1975):** CGB = 55% montmorillonite, 10–15% feldspar, 10% amorphous, 10% quartz, 5% calcite, 5–10% opal. CYB = 50% montmorillonite, 15% feldspar, 10–15% amorphous, 15% quartz, 5% calcite, 5% gypsum, <5% opal. **CEC**: CGB 65 meq/100g, CYB 71 meq/100g. **Untreated swelling index**: CGB 14.5 ml, CYB 15.5 ml — both **below** the API/TS EN ISO 13500 minimum of 22 ml, i.e. **both raw deposits genuinely fail the standard untreated**, directly analogous to the failure mode you're anticipating for raw Alaslaf clay if its Na/Ca ratio is low.

## Method

**Activation reagents:** soda ash (Na₂CO₃) and MgO, individually and in combination, at dosages from 0.5% to 4% by mass. **Critical methodological detail for your own lab protocol**: the authors found the **optimum moisture content for soda activation to be 40%** (established via their own preliminary trials) — bentonite was pre-moisturized to this level before soda addition, then **kneaded, then left to dry/cure under sunlight for a full month** before being crushed back to size and tested. MgO, by contrast, was added without any pre-moisturizing step. **This month-long curing period is substantially longer than the 12h/8h soak minimums currently in your own lab protocol** — worth flagging as an open question for your Phase 0/1 protocol: this paper doesn't test shorter curing windows, so it can't tell you whether your much shorter soak time is sufficient, only that a full month is what worked here.

**Testing:** swelling index (2g dried bentonite, 100 ml graduated cylinder, 5–6h settling); FANN viscosity at 6% mass slurry (11,000 rpm blend 10 min, 24h rest, 300/600 rpm readings); API filtration loss (7.5 and 30 min, only 30-min value used in analysis); electrokinetic/zeta-potential measurements on filtrate + filter-cake particles; and separate **coagulation tests** to determine the critical coagulation concentration (ccc) of MgO via visual settling inspection of dilute (0.025% solids) dispersions.

**Benchmark standard cited: API/TS EN ISO 13500 — 30 cP minimum viscosity @600 rpm, 15 cm³ maximum filtration loss, 22 ml minimum swelling index.** These numbers are consistent with the API 13A specification already in your reference base — useful independent confirmation citation for your Chapter 1/3 benchmark table.

## Key results

**CGB:** soda or MgO **individually** produced no significant viscosity improvement. The **combination of soda + MgO produced a clear synergy** — viscosity rose sharply with combined dosage, reaching 150–220 cP depending on ratio (Fig. 2), while soda-only activation (despite not raising viscosity much) gave the **lowest filtration losses** of any single treatment. Soda concentrations above 0.5% *within* a soda–MgO combination were found to **deteriorate** the technological properties (a real optimum, not "more is better"). **Recommended optimal formulation: 2–3% soda + 0.5% MgO**, yielding filtration loss 12–14 ml, viscosity 40–60 cP, swelling index 27–28 ml — **comfortably meeting the API/TS EN ISO 13500 standard** from a raw material that failed untreated.

**CYB:** optimum single-additive dosage for both soda and MgO alone was ~2%; beyond 2%, viscosity plateaued or declined. Combined soda (>1.5%) + MgO reached viscosity ~150 mPa·s (2% soda being "most convenient"). **Recommended formulation for CYB: 2% soda + 0.5% MgO** → filtration loss 15–16 ml, viscosity 65–70 mPa·s, swelling 31–32 ml — again meeting standard.

**Mechanistic explanation (Sections 3.2–3.3, this is the paper's core value for your Chapter 2):** the authors attribute **soda's effect primarily to ion exchange** (Na⁺ replacing interlayer Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺, promoting osmotic swelling and viscosity — the mechanism your thesis already relies on from Khan 2017 and similar sources), while **MgO's effect is attributed primarily to heterocoagulation**: positively-charged MgO species (Mg²⁺, MgOH⁺, or precipitated Mg(OH)₂(s), depending on pH) agglomerate with negatively-charged clay particle edges. **This is a genuinely two-sided, dosage-and-pH-dependent mechanism, not a simple "more MgO = better" relationship**: MgOH⁺ formation (moderate pH, moderate dosage) builds a gel-like structure that *raises* viscosity, while Mg(OH)₂(s) precipitation (higher pH/dosage) instead *destroys* the clay network via heterocoagulation, *lowering* viscosity and swelling. The isoelectric point of the bentonite–MgO system is reported at approximately **pH 12** (Fig. 11); across the pH 6–12 suspension range, bentonite and MgO particles carry opposite charges, driving the heterocoagulation.

**Critical coagulation concentration (ccc, Fig. 12):** at MgO concentrations above ~1% (CGB, both MgO-only and MgO+soda) or 1–2% (CYB), the bentonite suspension begins to visibly settle/coagulate — i.e. **there is an upper dosage limit beyond which added MgO actively destabilizes the suspension**, a hard mechanistic ceiling on how much MgO co-activation can help. The authors also note ccc values are **solids-concentration-dependent** (their coagulation tests ran at 0.025% solids vs. 6% solids for the viscosity tests) — a methodological detail worth remembering if you ever run an analogous low-concentration settling/flocculation characterization alongside your main-strength rheology tests.

**Na⁺ vs. Mg²⁺ directional effects, stated plainly (Section 3.3):** increasing Na⁺ concentration clearly increased both viscosity and swelling index while decreasing filtration loss — the textbook Na-activation signature your thesis is built around. Divalent cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺), by contrast, restrict swelling to four-water-layer complexes rather than the fuller osmotic swelling Na⁺ enables — direct mechanistic support for your own Na/Ca-ratio-driven go/no-go framework.

- Untreated CGB/CYB swelling indices (14.5/15.5 ml) fall below the 22 ml API/TS EN ISO 13500 minimum — both raw deposits fail the standard, directly analogous to your own anticipated raw-Alaslaf scenario
- Optimum activation formulation for both bentonites: **2–3% soda + 0.5% MgO**, achieving viscosity 40–70 cP, filtration loss 12–16 ml, swelling index 27–32 ml (all within API/TS EN ISO 13500 spec)

- Optimum moisture content for soda activation empirically determined at 40%; curing period used was a full month under sunlight before testing

## Caveats / limitations

1. **No RSM/DOE statistical design** — this is one-factor-at-a-time and paired-combination screening (varying soda % and MgO % independently and in fixed-ratio combinations, reading off graphs), not a Box-Behnken or CCD design. Don't cite this alongside your Afolabi/Ossai/Alhajabdalla/Asmungi/Satiyawira RSM sources as if it uses comparable statistical methodology — no regression equations, R², or ANOVA are reported anywhere in this paper.
2. **Long curing time not decomposed.** The month-long sunlight curing period is a fixed protocol choice, not a tested variable — the paper offers no data on whether shorter curing (e.g., your own 12h/8h) would under-perform. Don't extrapolate a specific minimum curing time from this paper; it only tells you what worked at one (long) duration.
3. **MgO is explicitly called "the most controversial additive" by the authors themselves** (abstract) — its dual/reversible mechanism (viscosity-enhancing at moderate dosage/pH, network-destroying above the ccc) makes it a genuinely more complex additive to reason about than soda alone. If your own thesis stays with a soda(Na₂CO₃)-only activation protocol (as currently planned), this paper's MgO-specific findings are mechanistic/contextual background rather than a direct procedural template.
4. **Coagulation (ccc) tests run at very low solids concentration (0.025%)** vs. viscosity/swelling tests at 6% solids — the authors themselves note ccc is solids-concentration-dependent, so the specific ccc percentages (1–2% MgO) shouldn't be assumed to transfer directly to your own planned working concentrations without adjustment.
5. **Turkish deposit, no Yemen/regional geological connection** — purely a mechanism/activation-chemistry precedent, not a local-clay-benchmarking or Middle-East comparator source, despite some structural similarity in narrative ("raw mixed bentonite brought to standard via activation").
6. Two-bentonite (CGB/CYB) comparison, both from the same mine/company — not an independent multi-deposit corroboration in the way your Nigeria/Saudi/Egypt comparator set is; treat both results together as one deposit-level case study, not two independent confirmations.
